# Week 11: Human evolution and the future of cooperation

*A reflective essay translated from the final report, rather than a simulation study.*

## 1. How might humans evolve?

Humanity's future requires distinguishing biological evolution from changes in technology and society. Systems beyond biology can exhibit evolutionary dynamics when they involve replication, transmitted variation, and selection. Here "culture" includes technologies, institutions, ideas, and religions.

Future human evolution should be considered in interaction with culture. Culture reshapes selective environments: medicine can reduce the extent to which some illnesses or physical characteristics determine survival and reproduction. Social norms affect reproduction too. Restrictions on polygamy can influence its distribution, while celibate religious practitioners can transmit culture without having children. Cultural transmission and biological reproduction need not favor the same outcomes.

Humans may continue changing alongside the systems they create, though specific outcomes are difficult to predict. AI offers one example. It may make specialized knowledge more accessible, allowing people without programming experience to build applications. Work previously divided among people may instead be divided between people and AI.

Whether this improves well-being is uncertain. Extensive automation could make meaningful work harder to find, or it could help address existing problems. These are possible scenarios, not predictions established by a model here.

## 2. What future should we work toward?

Evolution does not inherently move toward a desirable goal or have a direction of progress. Changing selection pressures does not guarantee an intended outcome. I interpret this question as asking which social changes would be desirable, rather than prescribing biological change. Claims about supposedly superior humans risk becoming eugenic arguments.

I would like cultural change to expand cooperation. Humans have benefited from cooperation among unrelated individuals, exchange, division of labor, and accumulated knowledge. Cooperation across groups and with future generations remains difficult. Environmental problems, infectious disease, war, and AI governance need institutions extending beyond one country or generation.

Reputation helps sustain cooperation, but a bad reputation can make constructive re-entry difficult. Society should discourage betrayal while preserving opportunities for apology, correction, and renewed participation.

Ideas that spread easily are not necessarily beneficial. Exclusionary ideologies or addictive services can attract attention while useful information spreads slowly. Institutions should help preserve long-term benefits instead of simply rewarding popularity.

The same applies to AI. Depending entirely on a few foundation models or information infrastructures may make society fragile. Multiple sources of judgment and mutual oversight could improve resilience.

The priority is to evaluate and improve the institutions and technologies humans create, and to build societies capable of cooperating across groups and generations.
